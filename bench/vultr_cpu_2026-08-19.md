# Vultr CPU candidates, measured 2026-08-19

Same benchmark that reports 23.83 ms/move on the DigitalOcean droplet, run on
each candidate in Newark (ewr), then all three destroyed. Total cost $0.24.

`ms/move` is one process on an otherwise idle box — the latency a visitor feels.
`moves/s` is N independent worker **processes**, which is how move serving
actually runs; threads do not scale (0.94x at 8 on real cores).

| plan | CPU | cores | $/mo | ms/move | 1 proc | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `vhf-3c-8gb`       | Intel Skylake       | 3 | 48 | **11.46** | 94.6 | 165.1 | 237.5 | — |
| `vhp-4c-8gb-amd`   | AMD EPYC-Rome       | 4 | 48 | 14.53 | 67.0 | 140.2 | 208.8 | **279.1** |
| `vhp-4c-8gb-intel` | Intel Cascadelake   | 4 | 48 | 14.48 | 71.0 | 134.9 | 197.7 | 257.0 |

Reference: DO droplet 23.83 ms/move (1 vCPU), Apple M1 core 5.66 ms/move.

## What the numbers say

**vhf wins per core at every equal process count** — 94.6 vs 67.0 at one, 237.5
vs 208.8 at three. Roughly 40% more work per core. `vhp` only wins on aggregate
by owning a fourth core.

**Process scaling on cloud vCPUs is near-linear**, which the Mac did not show:
AMD goes 67.0 -> 279.1 across four processes (4.17x) where the M1 managed 2.34x
across eight, because four of the M1's "cores" are efficiency cores. The
process-per-core architecture is worth more on this hardware than the Mac
measurements suggested.

## Chosen: `vhp-4c-8gb-amd`

Not because it is fastest per core — it is not — but because the main server
process needs a core of its own. On the 3-core vhf, main + 2 workers saturates
the box with nothing left for the identify fallback, ssh, or a burst. On the
4-core AMD, main + 3 workers fits, and main + 2 workers leaves a spare.

The per-move latency gap that argues for vhf is 3 ms. It sits behind ~100 ms of
Cloudflare round trip, so no visitor can perceive it. Queueing delay under load
is measured in seconds, and that is decided by aggregate throughput.

6 TB of bandwidth against vhf's 4 TB is a minor bonus.
